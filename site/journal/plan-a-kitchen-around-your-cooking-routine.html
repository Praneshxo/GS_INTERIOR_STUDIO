THE JOURNAL / PLANNING / 03

Plan a kitchen around your cooking routine

Describe an ordinary working day

A kitchen discussion becomes clearer when you explain a normal day at home. Mention who prepares breakfast, how many people cook together and when the main meal is made. Tell the designer whether you regularly use large vessels, keep several appliances on the counter or prepare food for visiting family. These habits help the team understand the space you need before choosing the colour of the shutters.

Explain what currently feels inconvenient. You may find that frequently used items are difficult to reach, the counter is crowded, or storage is spread across too many places. Specific examples are useful. “I need to put the mixer away after every use because there is no working space” describes a problem that the layout can address. “I want a modern kitchen” leaves that practical need hidden.

Make a realistic storage list

Group what you keep into everyday vessels, occasional serving items, dry groceries, cleaning supplies and appliances. Mention items bought in larger quantities and items that need particular handling according to their labels. The designer can then discuss suitable storage arrangements without guessing what your household owns. There is no benefit in creating many attractive compartments if the objects you use do not fit them.

Consider how you access things as well as how much you store. Tell the designer if a user finds bending, stretching or lifting difficult. Let the relevant professionals check proposed dimensions and clearances at the site. A layout shown in a reference photograph may not suit the room or the people using it. The final arrangement should be checked with your actual requirements, not accepted only because it looks familiar.

Confirm appliances before finalising the drawing

List the appliances you will retain and those you plan to buy. Share model information when available. Ask the designer to coordinate installation requirements with the appliance supplier and the relevant trades. Ventilation, services and access for maintenance need proper checking. A cabinet drawing should not depend on an approximate appliance size once manufacturing decisions are being made.

If a purchase is still undecided, mark it clearly. Agree what must be chosen before the drawing can be frozen and what flexibility, if any, remains afterwards. Buying a different appliance late in the project may affect surrounding work. Discuss that possibility before placing orders rather than expecting the finished unit to accommodate every future model.

Review the kitchen as a working space

Look at the proposed plan while imagining a meal being prepared. Where do ingredients come out? Where are vessels placed after washing? Can two people use the room without constantly moving around each other? Where do grocery bags arrive? These questions help you review the arrangement in a way that a colour presentation alone cannot. Ask for clarification when a drawing is difficult to understand.

Discuss cleaning and maintenance with the proposed material and hardware suppliers. Ask what routine care is required and what cleaners or methods should be avoided for the chosen products. Do not assume every surface carrying the same colour name behaves identically. Keep care instructions with your handover documents and make sure everyone who cleans the kitchen can understand them.

Finish with a clear decision record

Before approval, confirm the drawing version, appliance list, finish references, included accessories and work that belongs to other trades. Mark unresolved points rather than approving a drawing with hidden assumptions. A well-recorded decision gives the design team, installer and homeowner the same starting information. It also makes a later change easier to explain and price.
