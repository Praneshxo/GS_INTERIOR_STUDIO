THE JOURNAL / PLANNING / 04

A wardrobe brief that goes beyond the outer doors

Count the kinds of storage you need

The inside of a wardrobe should reflect what you own. Separate hanging clothes, folded clothes, sarees, occasional wear, bags, luggage and smaller accessories in your brief. Different family members may need different arrangements. There is no need to photograph every personal item. A simple list describing quantities and storage habits can give the designer useful information while keeping the discussion comfortable.

Explain what fails in your current storage. Perhaps shelves become untidy because too many items share one space. Perhaps everyday clothes are behind occasional luggage. Perhaps two people need access at the same time. These observations are more useful than asking for the largest wardrobe possible. A larger unit can still be inconvenient if its internal arrangement does not suit the way you use it.

Think about the surrounding bedroom

Review the wardrobe with the bed, doorway, window, dressing area and other furniture shown in the same plan. An attractive door style should also work within the available room. Ask the designer to show the access space and opening movement in a way you understand. Final measurements and product requirements need checking before the door system or internal fittings are ordered.

Tell the team about furniture you will keep. A bed with storage may need its own access space. A chair or cot may change the route across the room. Do not approve the wardrobe in isolation and discover these relationships after installation. The bedroom should remain easy to use once all its furniture is in place.

Separate daily access from occasional access

Discuss which items are needed each day and which can be placed in less frequently used areas. If reaching high shelves is difficult for someone, mention it in the brief. Where specialised fittings are proposed, ask to understand how they operate and what maintenance they require. Choose from demonstrated requirements rather than adding an accessory simply because it appears in a premium package.

Leave a clear place for items that do not fit the usual categories. Bags, long garments and luggage can easily be forgotten in an early drawing. Ask what happens when a family member's storage needs change. Some flexibility may be possible in the chosen system, but the designer should explain it specifically. Do not assume every shelf can be adjusted or every fitting can be changed later.

Review details before approval

Check the number and arrangement of drawers, shelves and hanging areas against your list. Confirm whether internal lighting, mirrors and organisers are included or separate. Review visible finishes using the approved sample process and record the reference. A photograph on a phone is useful for discussion, but it may not show the exact appearance of a physical finish under your room lighting.

Ask how handles, locks and other selected hardware will be documented. Where child access or a particular household need affects the choice, bring it to the design discussion for appropriate assessment. The written scope should identify the agreed items clearly enough that both sides can recognise the approved result.

Make handover useful

At handover, ask for a demonstration of the installed doors, drawers and any special fittings. Record issues through the agreed process and keep the care guidance. Take time to organise the wardrobe according to the plan before deciding that more storage is necessary. A clear internal arrangement works best when the household understands the purpose of each area.
